How a run works

Four steps, and only one of them is ours to decide.

The order matters. Calculation is arithmetic, review is judgement, and the commit is a person taking responsibility. Submission only happens after that.

STEP 1

Calculate

Gross to net for every employee. PAYE, National Insurance, pensions, student loans, statutory pay.

STEP 2

Review

Exceptions raised with the evidence behind them, so you can disagree with the system.

STEP 3

Commit

A named person signs off. The run cannot proceed while any exception is undecided.

STEP 4

Submit

FPS and EPS to HMRC, payment file to BACS, contributions to the pension provider.

Where most systems slip

  • Pension arrangements are not interchangeable: net pay reduces taxable pay only, salary sacrifice reduces National Insurance too, relief at source reduces neither
  • Student loan deductions round down to the whole pound; PAYE rounds down to the penny
  • Directors' NI is annual and cumulative, not monthly
  • An S-prefixed tax code uses Scottish bands regardless of where the employer sits

Leave held in hours, not days

Day-based entitlement breaks the moment someone works 22.5 hours over three days. After Harpur Trust v Brazel and the 2024 accrual reforms, part-year and irregular-hours staff are where tribunals happen. Hours survive a change of contract mid-year; days do not.

One system, two very different payrolls

A council pension and a workplace pension are not the same sum.

The differences between sectors are in the arithmetic, not the wording. Choosing the wrong earnings basis produces a number that looks plausible and is wrong every period.

Private companies

  • Auto-enrolment on qualifying earnings — a band, not the whole salary
  • Employment Allowance offset against employer NI
  • Directors on the annual NI basis
  • Weekly and fortnightly payrolls
  • Small employer relief on statutory payment recovery

Public bodies

  • LGPS and Teachers' schemes on pensionable pay
  • Employment Allowance generally not claimable
  • Multiple concurrent assignments with aggregated NI
  • Backdated national pay awards
  • Untaken leave as a balance-sheet liability

    What stays manual whatever you choose

    Committing a run, releasing a payment to a leaver, duplicate bank accounts, negative net pay.     Continuity

    Payroll does not wait for your payroll manager to get better.

    Most organisations have one person who really understands the payroll. When they are off on the 26th, the run still has to go out. The usual answer is to hope, or to delay everyone's pay.

    The first officer, not no pilot

    An autopilot does not fly an empty cockpit. It flies with someone qualified who can take control, and who is accountable for the flight. Cover mode is the same arrangement: it does more of the work, so a competent colleague can finish the run safely without being the person who knows every quirk of your payroll.

    Three things change

    • Routine corrections apply themselves, so there is less to get wrong
    • Every remaining decision is explained in plain English — what the risk is, not just what the rule found
    • A delegated limit caps what one person can release alone; above it, a second name is required

    What a stand-in actually sees

    "Someone has left but is still being paid." If you release this, the money goes and getting it back means asking them to return it. Hold it unless you know for certain they are still employed.

    Who is accountable stays clear

    Every decision is recorded as made under cover authority, naming both the person covering and the person they are deputising for. When the payroll lead returns they get a handover summary of everything decided in their absence — not a surprise in next month's reconciliation.
